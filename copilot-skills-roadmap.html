STAGE 0

Understand

What is Copilot, and when should I use it?
warm-up
What Copilot Is
01 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
What Microsoft Copilot actually is
Copilot is Microsoft's AI assistant built into Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, Teams, and Windows — not a separate product you have to go find, but a layer over tools you already use. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — choosing the right surface for two different tasks, not just naming one.
Copilot Chat lives in a sidebar or web page. Copilot inside Word/Excel/PPT lives right in the ribbon of that app. Same underlying AI, different entry points depending on where you're working.
02 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
What Copilot can and can't do
Copilot is strong at drafting, summarizing, and reformatting things you already have — it's weak at knowing anything outside what you give it or your organization's data, and it can't take actions you haven't explicitly allowed. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — correcting a colleague's wrong assumption about what Copilot can see.
Can: summarize a 40-page report in 5 bullets. Can: draft a first-pass email. Can't: know what happened in a meeting it wasn't grounded in. Can't: send an email on your behalf unless an agent action has been set up to do so.
03 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
How Copilot differs from a search engine
A search engine hands you a list of links to read yourself; Copilot reads the material for you and gives you a synthesized answer — faster, but only as reliable as its sources and your verification habit. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding which of two answers to trust and why.
Search: “budget delay policy” → 10 links to skim. Copilot: “What's our policy on budget delays?” grounded in #PolicyHandbook.docx → a direct answer with a citation you can click to confirm.
04 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Copilot Chat vs. Copilot inside Microsoft 365 apps
Copilot Chat is a general-purpose conversation surface; in-app Copilot is scoped to the document, sheet, or deck you have open — mixing them up is the single most common beginner confusion. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — catching yourself mid-task using the wrong surface.
Need to ask about several files, or something outside a specific document? Use Copilot Chat. Need to rewrite a paragraph you're staring at in Word right now? Use in-app Copilot in the ribbon.
Prompting Basics
05 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
What grounding means, at a basic level
Grounding is telling Copilot exactly what to base its answer on, instead of letting it guess — the single habit that separates a made-up-sounding answer from a trustworthy one. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing why a real prompt is producing vague answers.
Ungrounded: “Summarize our Q3 numbers.” (Copilot has to guess which file.) Grounded: “Summarize our Q3 numbers from #Q3Financials.xlsx.” (No guessing involved.)
06 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
What a prompt actually is
A prompt is just the instruction you type — but a one-line instruction and a well-structured one produce very different quality answers, which is the whole reason prompting is a skill worth learning. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — explaining the mechanism behind why specificity wins, not just picking the better prompt.
Weak prompt: “write something about the meeting.” Stronger prompt: “Write a 3-sentence recap of yesterday's planning meeting for teammates who missed it.”
Using Copilot Responsibly
07 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
AI limitations and hallucinations, in plain terms
Copilot can sound completely confident while being wrong — “hallucination” just means it generated something plausible-sounding that isn't actually true, which happens more often with vague or ungrounded prompts. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — telling two confident-sounding answers apart on reliability, not tone.
Asking a grounded question about a specific file rarely hallucinates. Asking a broad, ungrounded question (“what's our company's return policy?” with no source) is exactly where you're most likely to get a confident-sounding guess.
08 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Basic privacy and data awareness
Copilot at work is generally designed to respect your existing permissions, so it shouldn't surface content you couldn't otherwise access — but the exact behavior depends on your tenant's configuration and Copilot version, so don't assume it blindly. Either way, anything you paste into it is worth a second thought if it's sensitive, since organizational tools can log and retain prompts. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — spotting the gap in a colleague's reasonable-sounding but incomplete logic.
Fine to paste: a public product description you want reworded. Worth a pause before pasting: unreleased financials, personal employee data, or anything under an NDA with a third party.
09 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
How to verify an answer before you trust it
Grounded answers are generally expected to include a citation you can check — though whether one appears can depend on the app, data source, and Copilot version, so treat "no citation" as a reason for extra caution rather than proof there's nothing to check. When a citation is there, clicking it takes seconds and is the difference between confidently repeating a fact and confidently repeating a mistake. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether to let an unverified claim slide.
Before using a Copilot number or claim anywhere that matters (a client deck, a decision, a report), click the citation and confirm it actually says what Copilot claimed it says.
10 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Knowing when not to use Copilot
Copilot is a tool for drafting and synthesizing, not a substitute for judgment on legal, medical, HR, or high-stakes decisions — knowing where the line is matters as much as knowing how to prompt well. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether a specific high-stakes use case crosses the line.
Good fit: drafting a first-pass status update. Poor fit: making a final call on a legal contract clause, a medical question, or a sensitive HR decision without a qualified human reviewing it.
Exit criteria

You are ready for Prompt when you can:

  • Explain, in your own words, what Copilot can and can't do
  • Write a grounded prompt that references a real file with #
  • Verify an answer before trusting it — e.g. check a citation
  • Name one situation where you'd choose not to use Copilot at all

Prompt Craft
01 ⚪ Not started 15–20 min tap ▸
The GCSE prompt frame
The single structural habit that turns a vague ask into a usable first draft — skip a part and Copilot guesses, usually wrong. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing what's missing from a real prompt, not just building one from scratch.
Goal — what you actually want
Context — background Copilot doesn't already know
Source — the file/email/meeting to ground it in
Expectations — format, length, tone

"Draft a client update email (Goal) about the Q3 delay (Context), based on #ProjectTimeline.xlsx (Source), under 150 words, direct tone (Expectations)."
Anatomy of a grounded prompt
02 ⚪ Not started 10–15 min tap ▸
Grounding with # references
# pins Copilot to a specific file, email, or site instead of letting it guess at your intent from thin air — the difference between a real answer and a plausible-sounding one. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding what will go wrong before it happens, not just after.
# a file: #Q3Budget.xlsx
# a person's recent work: #Priya
# a whole site: #TeamSite

You can combine sources: “Compare #Q3Budget.xlsx to #Q2Budget.xlsx and flag categories that grew more than 15%.”
03 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Verifying citations before you trust the answer
Copilot typically cites its sources inline when it's grounding an answer in your organization's content — treating that citation as a link to click, not a stamp of truth, is what separates safe use from an embarrassing meeting mistake. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding what to do when verification itself breaks.
Grounded answers usually show numbered references (exact presentation can vary by app and version). Click through on anything that will go in front of a client, a number that will drive a decision, or a claim you'll repeat as fact.
04 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Iterative refinement over one giant prompt
A first prompt gets you 70% there — a short, specific follow-up gets the rest, faster than trying to anticipate every requirement up front. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing why a real overloaded prompt failed, not just avoiding one.
First: “Draft an agenda for tomorrow's budget review.”
Follow-up: “Make it 5 items, add 10-minute time boxes, move Q&A to the end.”
Follow-up: “Now write a one-line Teams message inviting the team, referencing the agenda.”
05 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Choosing the right Copilot surface
Copilot Chat, in-app Copilot, and Teams meeting Copilot each see different data — picking the wrong one means Copilot literally can't answer the question. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — talking a teammate out of a workaround that looks reasonable but isn't.
Copilot Chat (Work) — your organization's files, emails, chats
Copilot Chat (Web) — general internet questions, no work grounding
In-app Copilot (Word/Excel/PPT) — the open document only, by default
Teams meeting Copilot — only that meeting's transcript
Where Copilot Lives
06 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Setting tone, length & format explicitly
Copilot's default output is a safe, generic paragraph — naming the shape you want up front skips a whole round of reformatting. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing inconsistency across repeated attempts, not just writing one good prompt.
“...as a 3-column table.”
“...in under 100 words.”
“...casual tone, like a Slack message to a peer.”
“...as a numbered checklist, no more than 6 items.”
07 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Prompt anti-patterns to avoid
The three habits that quietly waste the most re-prompts: vague verbs, no source, and stacking unrelated asks in one message. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — restructuring someone else's overloaded prompt, not just avoiding one yourself.
Vague: “Help me with this report.”
Better: “Shorten this report's executive summary to 3 sentences.”

Stacked: “Summarize this, then write an email, then make a deck.”
Better: three separate, sequential prompts — see A4.
Building Robust Prompt Specifications
08 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Giving Copilot a role or persona
Assigning a role narrows tone, vocabulary, and depth of an answer more reliably than a bare adjective — Copilot writes differently as “a concise technical reviewer” than with no role at all. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing inconsistent results and explaining the fix, not just naming the technique.
“Act as a skeptical editor reviewing this proposal — flag anything vague or unsupported before you improve the wording.”
09 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Giving Copilot examples (few-shot prompting)
Showing one or two examples of the output format you want removes far more ambiguity than describing the format in words — Copilot pattern-matches on your example instead of guessing at a description. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing why a described format is still failing, not just picking the better method.
“Rewrite these three headlines in the same style as this one: ‘Q3 Revenue Climbs 12% on Strong Renewals.’”
10 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Specifying an output schema
For anything downstream — a table you'll paste into a sheet, a block a script will parse — naming the exact fields and order removes an entire round of manual reformatting. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing a broken downstream script, not just naming the fix in the abstract.
“Return each product as: Name | Price | In Stock (Yes/No) — one row per product, no extra commentary.”
11 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Setting acceptance criteria
Telling Copilot what “done” looks like — length, must-include items, what to exclude — gives you something concrete to check the output against, instead of eyeballing whether it “feels right.” Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — resolving a real disagreement about whether something's good enough.
“This draft is done when it: mentions the Q3 renewal number, stays under 150 words, and doesn't use the word ‘synergy.’”
12 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Asking Copilot to flag missing information
Copilot will often fill a gap with a plausible guess instead of telling you the gap exists — explicitly asking it to call out what's missing surfaces problems before they reach your reader. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether to trust a suspiciously clean-looking answer.
“Draft this incident summary from the notes below, and list anything you needed to guess or that's missing before I can call this complete.”
13 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Asking Copilot to state its uncertainty
A one-line confidence check costs nothing and catches some of the answers that are technically ungrounded guesses dressed up in confident-sounding language. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — knowing when a confidence label isn't enough on its own.
“Answer the question, then add one line: how confident are you in this, and why?”
14 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Prompting for comparison
Comparison tasks — which vendor, which draft, which option — go better with an explicit structure; naming the criteria up front stops Copilot from picking its own, possibly irrelevant, points of comparison. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing a comparison that went wrong, not just avoiding the mistake.
“Compare Option A and Option B on cost, setup time, and support quality only — as a 2-column table.”
15 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Extraction vs. generation prompts
Asking Copilot to pull out or transform something already true in front of it is a fundamentally different task from asking it to invent new content — mixing the two into one vague prompt is where hallucination risk climbs. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether a specific blended request is actually safe.
Extraction: “List every deadline mentioned in this email thread — quote the exact date and email.” Generation: “Now draft a summary reminder based on those deadlines.”
Exit criteria

You are ready for Intermediate when you can:

  • Build a grounded prompt without a template
  • Select the correct Copilot surface for a task
  • Verify citations before trusting an answer
  • Refine an output in 2–3 turns
  • Explain when Copilot should not be used

Drafting from a blank page

Selecting a passage and asking Copilot to rewrite it is faster than starting over, and lets you target one specific problem at a time — tone, length, or reading level. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — talking a teammate out of a habit that feels thorough but wastes time.
Select a paragraph, then: “Make this more concise” or “Rewrite for a non-technical reader.”

Summarizing a long document
A 40-page contract or report collapses to the parts that matter — the skill is asking for the right length and focus, not just “summarize this.” Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing why two people got the wrong summary from the same kind of prompt.
“Summarize this document in 5 bullet points, focused on financial commitments.”
04 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Extracting specific answers
Copilot in Word can answer specific questions about a doc without you reading the whole thing — useful for a 40-page contract as much as a 4-page memo. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether a habitual two-step approach is actually the fastest one.
“What are the payment terms in this contract?”
“List every date-driven deadline mentioned in this document.”

Comparing two versions or sections
Asking Copilot to compare two drafts or two sections surfaces the substantive differences instead of you scanning line by line for what changed. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing a real comparison that returned too much noise.

For analysis a formula can't easily express — trendlines, correlations, forecasts — Copilot can write and run Python in the grid from a plain-English ask. PowerPoint Copilot

Generating a deck from a document or prompt

Cutting a 20-slide deck to a tight 10 is a judgment call Copilot can make a credible first pass at — keeping the data slides and dropping the filler — that you then review, not rebuild. 14 ⚪ Not started 10 min tap ▸
Drafting speaker notes

Connected Workflows & AI Analysis
week 3

Central concept: Workflow design

Researcher → Page → Word → PowerPoint isn't one trick to memorize — it's one instance of a repeating shape. Every reliable Copilot chain in this stage follows the same seven moves. Once you can name them, you can design a new chain instead of copying this one.

Input→ Grounding→ Transform→ Verify→ Human decision→ Output→ Store/share
Chat Agents
01 ⚪ Not started 20–25 min tap ▸
Copilot Researcher — deep multi-step reports
Researcher plans its own steps, searches web and work content, and shows its reasoning — built for questions that need synthesis across many sources, not a single lookup. The challenge below has real difficulty tiers, not just longer answers: Easier tests recognition (spot the well-formed prompt), Standard tests application (write one yourself, scored on 7 prompt dimensions — Goal, Context, Source, Audience, Constraints, Output format, Verification), and Harder tests judgment (handle conflicting sources, not just more keywords).
“Research our top 3 competitors' recent pricing changes and summarize the implications for our Q3 pricing strategy, citing sources.”

Good Researcher prompts name a decision the report should inform, not just a topic.
How a Researcher report gets built
02 ⚪ Not started 20 min tap ▸
Copilot Analyst — data analysis with a visible trace
Analyst works like a junior data analyst: it plans steps, writes and runs code, and shows its work — you can inspect and correct the reasoning, not just trust the final number. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application (7 prompt dimensions: Goal, Context, Source, Audience, Constraints, Output format, Verification), and Harder tests judgment — resolving a disagreement between Analyst's own outputs, not just adding keywords.
“Analyze this sales data and identify the 3 biggest drivers of the Q2 dip.”
“Build a cohort retention analysis from this signup data.”

Ask it to show its work: “Explain the steps you took to reach that conclusion.”
03 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Copilot Pages — turning chat into a shared doc
A useful Chat thread doesn't have to stay a chat — pinning it to a Page makes it a living, collaborative document others can edit alongside you. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application (7 prompt dimensions: Goal, Context, Source, Audience, Constraints, Output format, Verification), and Harder tests judgment — reconciling conflicting edits without silently picking a side.
From any Copilot Chat response: “Save this to a Page.”
Then invite teammates to co-edit, or ask Copilot within the Page to keep refining the content.
04 ⚪ Not started 20 min tap ▸
Chaining multiple Copilot skills into one workflow
Research → draft → deck isn't three unrelated tasks — it's one workflow, and naming the handoff between steps is what makes the chain actually save time. Mapped to the workflow-design shape above: Input (the open question) → Grounding (Researcher's sources) → Transform (Word draft, then PowerPoint deck) → Verify (you check citations and claims) → Human decision (you approve the brief before it becomes a deck) → Output (the deck) → Store/share (saved to a Page, sent to stakeholders). Each step below is scored on 7 prompt dimensions (Goal, Context, Source, Audience, Constraints, Output format, Verification) rather than keywords alone.
1. Researcher: “Research X and summarize the implications.”
2. Save the result to a Page.
3. Word: “Draft a brief using the findings on [Page link].”
4. PowerPoint: “Create a presentation from that brief.”

  • Use Copilot Analyst with a visible reasoning trace
  • Convert findings into a shareable deliverable — a brief, a deck, or a Page

Copilot Studio basics — topics & triggers
An agent is a set of topics, each with trigger phrases and a conversation flow — building one starts with mapping the questions people will actually ask, not the UI. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — resolving a real trigger collision, not just listing more phrases.

Grounding an agent with knowledge sources

Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding what not to ground the agent in, not just what to add.
Add knowledge: a SharePoint document library, a specific file, or a public URL.
The agent then answers from that source and can cite it, the same way Copilot Chat cites files with #.

Actions & connectors — agents that do, not just answer
An action lets an agent actually create a ticket, update a record, or send a notification — the line between an FAQ bot and something that does real work. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — designing a failure path, not just a happy path.
Add an action: connect to a system (e.g. a ticketing tool or a Power Automate flow), map the conversation's inputs to the action's parameters, define what success/failure looks like in the reply.
Anatomy of a Copilot Studio topic
04 ⚪ Not started 20 min tap ▸
Designing topics that don't collide
Two topics with overlapping trigger phrases fight over the same user message — the fix is distinct, specific phrasing per topic, tested against real user language. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — diagnosing a real collision from a test trace.
Colliding: “account help” triggers both “Reset password” and “Update billing”.
Fix: make triggers specific — “password”/“locked out” for one, “billing”/“invoice”/“card on file” for the other.

Testing & publishing an agent
An agent isn't done until it's tested against edge cases and published to where people actually work — Teams, a website, or another channel. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding what's actually safe to skip before publishing.
Test panel: run through your trigger phrases plus a few unexpected ones.
Publish: choose the channel (Teams, a website widget, etc.), set who can access it, then publish.
Systematic Evaluation
01 ⚪ Not started 25–30 min tap ▸
Agent evaluation — testing at scale
One test panel run tells you the agent works on the phrasing you tried. A test set — dozens of cases across normal, ambiguous, malformed, unauthorized, and out-of-date scenarios — tells you how it fails, and by how much. That's the difference between "I can build an agent" and "I can engineer and govern one."
Every row gets a Test, an Expected outcome, and — after running it — an Actual outcome. Comparing the two gives a Result. Do that across enough rows and six numbers fall out of the Result column.

Central concept: Agent Governance & Lifecycle

Every skill above this point answers "can I build this agent?" This one answers the question a real deployment actually turns on:

Can I build this agent?  →  Should we deploy this agent?
Ownership — who answers for the agent after it ships
Business owner
Owns the "why" — the process the agent serves, and whether it's still worth running.
Agent owner
Owns topics, triggers, and grounding — the person who actually maintains the build.
Data owner
Owns the knowledge sources — approves what's in scope and who can access it.

Access model→ Knowledge-source ownership→ Testing requirements→ Change management→ Versioning→ Monitoring→ Retirement / decommissioning
06 ⚪ Not started 15–20 min tap ▸
Security, permissions & sensitivity labels
Copilot is built to be permission-aware — surfacing only what the person asking already has access to — and understanding that model is what lets you trust it with sensitive data instead of avoiding it, while still knowing configuration and version can affect the details. This is the access-model piece of governance: who can reach the agent, and what it's allowed to see on their behalf. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — naming the real governance risk, not the obvious-sounding one.
Copilot is designed to respect existing permissions — it shouldn't show you a file you can't already open, though it's worth confirming against your org's own configuration.
Sensitivity labels (Confidential, Highly Confidential) can further restrict what Copilot will summarize or extract from a labeled file.
07 ⚪ Not started 15 min tap ▸
Responsible AI practices
Review-before-send, awareness of overreliance, and keeping a human in the loop on consequential decisions aren't compliance checkboxes — they're what keeps Copilot a tool instead of an unreviewed decision-maker. This is where testing requirements and change management earn their place in the lifecycle: nothing ships or changes without this review step. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding when autonomy is and isn't acceptable.
Before sending/publishing anything Copilot drafted: does this go to a client, drive a decision, or state a fact as certain? If yes, review it like you'd review a colleague's draft.
08 ⚪ Not started 20 min tap ▸
Measuring adoption & iterating
Usage analytics and user feedback tell you which topics people actually need versus which ones you guessed wrong on — an agent is a living thing you tune, not a one-time build. This is the monitoring stage of the lifecycle, and a business owner reading these numbers is how "should we keep running this?" gets answered instead of assumed. Easier tests recognition, Standard tests application, and Harder tests judgment — deciding whether a good-looking metric actually means the agent is done.
Copilot Studio analytics: sessions, top triggered topics, fallback rate (questions that hit no topic).
A high fallback rate on a specific phrase pattern is a signal to add a new topic or trigger.

Understand

Spot the surface mismatch

Scenario: A coworker is stuck in Copilot Chat asking “what does my open spreadsheet say about Q3?” and getting nowhere.
Explain in 1–2 sentences why this fails, and name the fix.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Copilot Chat isn't automatically scoped to the open spreadsheet. Fix: either use in-app Copilot inside Excel, or ground the Chat prompt explicitly with a # reference to the file.
Understand

Catch the ungrounded guess

Scenario: A teammate asks Copilot “what's our vacation policy?” with no file referenced, and gets a confident-sounding paragraph back.
Write the grounded version of this prompt, and explain why the ungrounded version is riskier.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
“What's our vacation policy, based on #HRHandbook.docx?” The ungrounded version forces Copilot to guess at a source, which is exactly the kind of prompt most likely to produce a confident-sounding but unverified answer.
Understand

Decide: use it, or don't

Scenario: Three requests land on your desk: drafting a team status update, finalizing the wording of a legal clause, and summarizing a 10-page vendor proposal.
Sort the three into “good fit for Copilot” vs. “needs a qualified human first,” with a one-line reason for each.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Good fit: status update draft (low stakes, easy to review) and proposal summary (synthesis task). Needs a human: the legal clause — high-stakes wording where Copilot's draft should never be the final word without qualified review.
Prompt

Turn a vague ask into a GCSE prompt

Scenario: Your manager says: “can you get Copilot to help with the vendor email” — that's it, that's the whole ask.
1) Write the GCSE prompt you'd actually type, inventing reasonable Goal/Context/Source/Expectations.
2) Write one follow-up prompt refining the tone.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Draft prompt:
“Draft an email to our vendor about the delayed shipment (Goal+Context), based on #VendorContract.docx (Source), 100 words, firm but professional (Expectations).”

Follow-up:
“Make the tone a bit warmer — we still want to keep working with them.”
Prompt

Ground a prompt in the right source

Scenario: You need a summary of what changed between this month's and last month's budget, and both files live in the same SharePoint folder.
Write a single # -grounded prompt comparing the two named files and asking for only the categories that moved.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
“Compare #Budget-October.xlsx to #Budget-September.xlsx and list only the categories that changed by more than $500.”
Prompt

Spot and fix a stacked prompt

Scenario: A colleague's prompt: “Summarize this report, then write a LinkedIn post about it, then translate the post to Spanish.”
Split this into 3 separate, sequential prompts — write all three.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
1) “Summarize this report in 4 bullet points.”
2) “Write a LinkedIn post based on that summary, under 100 words.”
3) “Translate that post to Spanish.”
Intermediate

Draft-then-refine a client email in Word

Scenario: You need to tell a client a delivery is delayed 2 weeks, but you don't want to sound like you're making excuses.
1) Write the first draft prompt.
2) Write one refinement prompt after reading the draft.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Draft:
“Draft an email to a client explaining a 2-week delivery delay, owning the delay without over-apologizing, offering a concrete next step.”

Refine:
“Cut the second paragraph — too much explanation. Get to the new date and next step faster.”
Intermediate

Excel Copilot: flag and summarize in one pass

Scenario: A sheet has columns Rep, Region, Sales, Target. You want underperformers flagged and a regional rollup.
Write one prompt (or two sequential ones) that both flags rows below target and builds a regional summary.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
“Add a column that flags rows where Sales is below Target.”
Follow-up: “Now build a PivotTable summarizing total Sales by Region.”
Intermediate

Deck from a doc, then trim it

Scenario: You have a 4-page Word proposal and need a 6-slide pitch deck for a 15-minute meeting tomorrow.
1) Write the prompt to generate the deck from the file.
2) Write the follow-up trimming/adjusting it for the time slot.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
“Create a 6-slide presentation from #Proposal.docx.”
Follow-up: “Cut slide 5 — we won't have time for pricing detail in a 15-minute meeting, just mention it's in the appendix.”
Advanced

Triage an inbox thread before a meeting

Scenario: You're walking into a meeting in 5 minutes and there's a 25-message thread you haven't read about the topic.
Write the single prompt that gets you meeting-ready fastest.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)

Advanced

Research prompt for a real decision

Scenario: Leadership wants to know if the company should offer a 4-day work week, and wants a recommendation, not just a topic overview.
Write a Researcher prompt that names the decision, not just the topic.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)

Advanced

Analyst prompt with a visible check step

Scenario: Support ticket volume spiked last month and you need to know why before the postmortem meeting.
1) Write the initial Analyst prompt.
2) Write the follow-up asking it to show its reasoning.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
“Analyze this ticket data and identify the top 3 drivers of last month's spike.”
Follow-up: “Walk me through exactly how you identified those 3 drivers.”
Expert

Design 3 non-colliding topics for a support agent

Scenario: You're building an internal IT-helpdesk agent that needs to handle password resets, software requests, and hardware issues — without the topics stepping on each other.
Write distinct trigger-phrase sets for all three topics, avoiding shared generic words.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Password reset: “reset password”, “locked out”, “can't log in”
Software request: “install software”, “request an app”, “need a license”
Hardware issue: “laptop broken”, “monitor not working”, “need a replacement device”
Expert

Scope a knowledge source for a new agent

Scenario: HR wants an FAQ agent, but the only SharePoint site available has 4 years of mixed HR, legal, and facilities documents.
Describe what you'd do before grounding the agent in that site, in 2–3 sentences.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Create or identify a curated folder/library containing only current, HR-specific policy documents. Ground the agent in that scoped source rather than the whole site, and archive or exclude outdated documents so the agent can't cite them.
Expert

Add an action with a clear failure path

Scenario: Your agent needs to create a support ticket in an external system when a user reports a bug.
Describe the 3 things you'd configure: the connection, the input mapping, and what happens on failure.
Reveal ideal prompt(s)
Connection: link the action to the ticketing system's connector.
Mapping: map the conversation's captured fields (description, severity, contact) to the ticket's required parameters.
Failure path: if the connector call fails, have the agent tell the user the ticket wasn't created and give them a fallback (e.g. an email address) rather than saying it succeeded.
